# Unit 1 Quick Reference — Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

Use this as a short revision guide for Unit 1. It summarises what we actually covered; it is not intended to introduce new material.

---

## At a glance

### Atomic structure
- **Proton:** charge +1, in nucleus.
- **Neutron:** charge 0, in nucleus.
- **Electron:** charge −1, occupies orbitals around the nucleus.
- **Atomic number (Z):** number of protons.
- **Mass number (A):** protons + neutrons.
- Neutral atom: protons = electrons.
- **Ion:** atom with unequal numbers of protons and electrons.
- **Isotopes:** same element (same proton number), different neutron numbers.

### Electron structure
Hierarchy:

**shell → subshell → orbital → electron**

| Subshell | Orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---:|---:|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |
| f | 7 | 14 |

Maximum electrons in shell (n):

**2n²**

So shells can contain 2, 8, 18, 32... electrons. This is capacity, not necessarily the order in which electrons fill.

### Filling rules
- **Aufbau:** fill the lowest-energy available orbitals first.
- **Madelung:** compare (n+l); lower value fills first. If tied, lower (n) fills first.
- **Hund:** equal-energy orbitals fill singly before electrons pair.
- **Pauli:** maximum two electrons per orbital; if paired, they have opposite spins.

Useful filling sequence:

**1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p ...**

### Periodic table
- **Groups:** columns.
- **Periods:** rows.
- **Period:** related to the highest occupied principal shell.
- **Block:** determined by the type of subshell being filled: s, p, d or f.
- Block widths follow orbital capacity: s = 2, p = 6, d = 10, f = 14.

### Main-group valence patterns
Examples:

- Group 1: (ns^1)
- Group 2: (ns^2)
- p-block patterns continue (ns^2np^1) through (ns^2np^6).

For main-group elements, valence electrons are the electrons in the outer occupied shell.

### Periodic trends: derive, don't just memorise
Think about three things:

**nuclear charge + distance + shielding**

Across a period →:
- nuclear charge increases;
- electrons are added broadly within the same principal shell;
- attraction to the nucleus generally strengthens;
- atomic radius generally decreases;
- ionisation energy generally increases;
- electronegativity generally increases.

Down a group ↓:
- extra occupied shells increase distance and shielding;
- outer electrons feel less attraction from the nucleus;
- atomic radius generally increases;
- ionisation energy generally decreases;
- electronegativity generally decreases.

# 1. Atoms, isotopes and ions

The identity of an element is determined by its **number of protons**.

If an atom has atomic number 8, it has 8 protons. A neutral atom therefore also has 8 electrons.

The number of neutrons is:

**neutrons = mass number − atomic number**

Changing the number of neutrons produces an **isotope**, not a different element.

Changing the number of electrons produces an **ion**:
- losing electrons → positive ion;
- gaining electrons → negative ion.

The nucleus attracts electrons through **electrostatic attraction** between opposite charges. This is not magnetism and not the strong nuclear force.

---

# 2. Bonding: the introductory picture

## Ionic bonding
Electrons are transferred, producing oppositely charged ions. The bond is the **electrostatic attraction between those ions**.

## Covalent bonding
Atoms **share electrons**.

The octet rule is a useful introductory pattern: many main-group atoms form arrangements associated with a full outer shell. It is a model, not a universal law.

## Electronegativity and bond polarity
**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a bond.

If two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the electrons are shared unequally and the bond becomes **polar**.

Ionic and covalent bonding are therefore not completely separate boxes; bonding can be thought of as a continuum of electron sharing/transfer.

---

# 3. Shells, subshells and orbitals

The old 2,8,8 shell model is useful, but incomplete.

A **shell** is a principal energy level labelled by (n=1,2,3,ldots). It is not a literal circular path.

Each shell contains one or more **subshells**:

- shell 1: 1s
- shell 2: 2s, 2p
- shell 3: 3s, 3p, 3d
- shell 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f

A subshell contains **orbitals**.

An orbital is a quantum state associated with a spatial probability distribution for finding an electron. It is not an orbit or a fixed electron trajectory.

For example, the p subshell contains three orbitals. Each can hold two electrons:

(p: [ ][ ][ ])

so a p subshell holds at most six electrons.

# 4. Building electron configurations

Example: sulfur has atomic number 16, so a neutral sulfur atom has 16 electrons.

Fill the lowest-energy available states:

**1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴**

The shell totals are therefore:

**2, 8, 6**

The detailed configuration explains where the simpler shell numbers come from.

For (3p^4), Hund's rule gives:

**[↑↓] [↑] [↑]**

rather than pairing electrons in two orbitals while leaving the third empty.

## Noble-gas notation
A noble-gas symbol can replace the completed inner configuration.

For example:

**[Ar] 4s² 3d¹**

means the atom contains all 18 electrons of argon's configuration, followed by (4s^2 3d^1).

---

# 5. Why filling order is not simply shell 1, then 2, then 3...

Subshell energies overlap.

The important example from Unit 1 is:

**4s fills before 3d.**

Using Madelung:

- 4s: (n+l=4+0=4)
- 3d: (n+l=3+2=5)

So 4s fills first in the introductory model.

This explains why potassium is:

**[Ar] 4s¹**

rather than putting electron 19 into 3d.

Scandium then becomes:

**[Ar] 4s² 3d¹**

Its shell totals are 2, 8, 9, 2 — showing directly why the simple 2,8,8 picture cannot be the full model.

---

# 6. Electron configurations explain the periodic table

The periodic table reflects electron structure.

## Blocks
The subshell being filled determines the block.

Because:
- s has 1 orbital → 2 electrons → s-block is 2 columns wide;
- p has 3 orbitals → 6 electrons → p-block is 6 columns wide;
- d has 5 orbitals → 10 electrons → d-block is 10 columns wide;
- f has 7 orbitals → 14 electrons → f-block is 14 columns wide.

## Period lengths
Period 1 fills 1s → **2 elements**.

Period 2 fills 2s + 2p → **2 + 6 = 8 elements**.

Period 3 fills 3s + 3p → **8 elements**. Although shell 3 can hold 18 electrons, 4s becomes energetically available before 3d is filled.

Period 4 fills:

**4s² 3d¹⁰ 4p⁶**

giving **18 elements**.

So a shell's maximum capacity and a period's length are not the same thing.

---

# 7. Why periodic trends happen

Avoid treating periodic trends as arrows to memorise. Ask what happens to the attraction between the nucleus and the relevant electrons.

## Across a period
Proton number increases, so nuclear charge increases.

The added electrons are broadly entering the same principal shell, so shielding does not increase enough to cancel the stronger nuclear charge.

Result: electrons are generally pulled closer and held more strongly.

Therefore, across a period:
- radius ↓
- ionisation energy ↑
- electronegativity ↑

## Down a group
Additional occupied shells place the outer electrons farther from the nucleus and introduce more shielding.

Even though the nucleus contains more protons, the outer electrons are generally less strongly attracted.

Therefore, down a group:
- radius ↑
- ionisation energy ↓
- electronegativity ↓

---

# 8. The 4s/3d nuance

The filling rule should not be treated as an immutable energy ladder.

Although 4s fills before 3d in the introductory filling sequence, once 3d is occupied the relative energies change. When transition-metal atoms form positive ions, **4s electrons are generally removed before 3d electrons**.

Example:

Fe: **[Ar] 4s² 3d⁶**

Fe²⁺: **[Ar] 3d⁶**

A useful physical intuition is that the 4s electrons are associated with the outer (n=4) shell and are easier to remove once the 3d subshell is occupied.

Detailed transition-metal group numbering and valence-electron chemistry were deliberately deferred.

---

# 9. Chromium and copper: limits of the simple filling rule

Madelung/Aufbau predicts useful filling patterns, but it is not a perfect fundamental law.

Simple prediction for chromium:

**[Ar] 4s² 3d⁴**

Observed ground-state configuration:

**[Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵**

Simple prediction for copper:

**[Ar] 4s² 3d⁹**

Observed:

**[Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰**

Half-filled (d^5) and filled (d^{10}) arrangements are particularly favourable in these cases.

The important lesson is not to memorise a long exception list. It is to understand that actual configurations minimise the atom's total energy, while rules such as (n+l) are useful predictive approximations.

# Common traps

**“Orbit” vs “orbital”**  
Electrons do not travel around the nucleus on fixed paths. Use **orbital**.

**Shell capacity vs filling order**  
Shell 3 can hold 18 electrons, but that does not mean all 18 fill before shell 4 begins.

**A p⁵ configuration does not mean five valence electrons**  
For a main-group atom with (ns^2np^5), both subshells are in the outer shell: **7 valence electrons**.

**4s fills first, but is also generally removed first**  
Do not simply reverse the filling sequence when making ions.

**Periodic trends are not unexplained arrows**  
Reconstruct them using **nuclear charge, distance and shielding**.

**Aufbau/Madelung is a model**  
Chromium and copper demonstrate that the simple filling rule is not exact.

**Do not overextend main-group rules to the d-block**  
Detailed transition-metal group and valence rules were not part of Unit 1 and should not be assumed yet.
