### Q4 — Ionisation energy down a group
**Question:** X is in period 2 and Y is in period 4 of the same group. A student says Y has more protons, so it must have higher first ionisation energy. Agree or disagree, and explain.

**Learner answer:**
> No I do not agree with the conclusion. If Y is in period 4 then there are 2 factors which will reduce the ionisation energy compared to X
>
> 1) Being in period 4 means that Y's highest level electron is in shell 4, further away than period 2 X who's highest electron is in shell 2. The electrostatic attraction from the nucleus is proportional to the inverse square of the distance, so this means the attraction will be considerably weaker for the shell 4 electrons in Y vs the shell 2 electrons in X
>
> 2) There will be increased shielding of the nucleus' attraction to the higher shell electrons in Y as there will be electons in shell 3 shielding the shell 4 electrons reducing the attraction force.
>
> These 2 factors outweigh the increased attraction force generated by Y having more protons

### Q6 — Bonding continuum
**Question:** Bond A joins atoms with identical electronegativity; bond B joins atoms with significantly different electronegativities. A student says B must be ionic and A must be covalent. Assess both claims, including whether there is a clear ionic–covalent boundary.

**Learner answer:**
> Ni, i don't agree with either statement.
>
> Electronegativity is the measure of the strength of an atoms pull on electrons. In case A where the atoms have equal electronegativity there will be an equal pull on shared electrons meaning the electrons will be shared equally, forming a covalent bond
>
> When there is a difference in electronegativity, the higher electronegative atom will have a larger share of the electrons. This is still a covalent bond but will be described as a polar covalent bond.
>
> If the difference is large enough the electons are not shared but instead are transferred to the higher electronegative atom. This causes 2 ions to form, one negatively charged and one positively charged which then attract each other strongly. This is a ionic bond.
>
> There is not a clear boundary for when a ionic bond happens, it is better to think of these bonds types as continuous

### Q7 — Third-shell capacity versus period length
**Question:** A student argues that because shell 3 holds 18 electrons, period 3 must contain 18 elements. Evaluate using subshell capacities and filling order.

**Learner answer:**
> No, their reasoning is not valid.
>
> It is true that the third shell can accommodate up to 18 electrons. This is because it contains 3 subshells (s,p,d), subshells contain orbitals which themselves can contain up to 2 electrons. S has 1 orbitals, p has 3, and d has 5. So the third shell can contain up to (1+3+5)*2 = 18 electrons
>
> In practice however electrons do not simply fill shells, subshells and orbitals in order, they seek to minimise energy and this can mean filling orbitals in higher shells. This is what limits the number of elements in periods 3+, when the energy to fill an orbital in a higher shell is lower than the energy to fill an orbital in the current shell.
>
> This happens in shell 3 when we get to the 19th electron. We fill up the first 18 electrons as follows 1s^2,2s^2,2p^6,3s^2,3p^6 but when we look to fill the 19th electron we can use the n+l rule to see which orbital will fill next, where n is the shell number and l is the subshell label
>
> Here we have two relevant options 3d: 3+2=5 and 4s: 4+0=4. The n+l rule tells us we should fill the lowest number first so here we fill the 4s orbital and begin the next period

### Corrections and nuances
1. **Group numbering:** Q5's “group 7” describes the seven-valence-electron main-group family in older terminology; modern IUPAC numbering is **Group 17**.
2. **Noble-gas shorthand:** Q8 should read `[Ar] 4s¹`; F3 showed this was a slip.
3. **Copper energetics:** A completely filled d subshell does not automatically outweigh all other energetic costs. The observed configuration minimises the *total* energy of the atom. F2 exposed a real gap and was taught after the answer.
4. **Orbitals:** An orbital is a quantum state associated with a spatial probability distribution, not a definite trajectory.
5. **Physical approximations:** In many-electron atoms, distance, shielding and the `n+l` rule are useful explanatory models, not exact calculations of electron energies.
